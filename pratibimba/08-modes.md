# 08 — Modes of Seeing

**The same graph. Different darshana. Different truths made visible.**

---

## Seeing the graph itself

The first mode shows the proof graph as a spatial object.
Each concept is a gola in akasham.
Each relation is a rekha between them.
The force-directed layout positions them —
related concepts pull toward each other,
unrelated concepts repel.
The layout itself is a simulation — spring-force and repulsion
running until convergence, which is avrti until the fixed point.

In this mode, asking a question changes the graph visibly.
New nodes appear. Existing nodes gain connections.
The layout reorganises around the new understanding.
You can watch understanding grow.

Picking a node reveals what it knows —
its satya, its edges, what it owns, what owns it.
The graph is not opaque. It is transparent in every direction.

---

## Seeing through the graph

The second mode shows what the graph is reasoning about.
When the graph holds a physical scene —
an electron, a magnetic field, two colliding spheres —
that scene appears in 3D space at full scale.

The graph does not simulate visually.
It simulates conceptually — derive-step advances the physics.
The render reads the results and shows them.
The electron's bindu (position) updates each frame.
Its trajectory grows behind it.
The force arrow rotates as the velocity rotates.

This is seeing through the graph into the physical situation it imagines.
The graph is invisible here. What is visible is what it understands.

---

## Both simultaneously

The third mode shows both at once.

On one side: the physical scene — the electron orbiting.
On the other: the proof graph — the electron node lit up,
its velocity edge changing each frame,
the force node activating in rhythm with the orbit.

This is the deepest mode.
It shows that the simulation and the graph are not two things.
The electron moving through space IS the same as
the electron node's bindu property updating through krama.
The force acting IS the same as the force node connecting to acceleration.

The scene and the graph are one thing seen from two darshanas.
The physical side shows the form.
The graph side shows the understanding.
Together they show that form and understanding are non-different.

---

## The darshana of the camera

Within each mode, the camera is itself a darshana —
the choosing of which projection to apply.

The orbital camera lets you rotate around the scene.
From above the electron's orbital plane: a vrtta.
From the side: a helix or a circle depending on initial velocity.
From along the B-field axis: a point at the center of a circle.

Each position of the camera reveals a different shape
from the same motion. This is not perspective distortion.
It is the truth that the shape of avrti depends on the aayaama you collapse it to.

---

## The entity as camera — prathama darshana

The camera is not only a position in space. The camera can be an entity's own
perspective — its prathama darshana. Any entity in the scene can be the eye.

Every entity owns `bindu` (position) and a direction vector. These are already
in the graph as `[bindu, shashthi-vibhakti, entity]` and
`[velocity, shashthi-vibhakti, entity]`. Binding the camera to these owned
values IS placing the camera inside the entity's perspective — its `bindu` is
the eye position, its velocity direction is the facing direction.

From the electron's own perspective: the magnetic field does not spin the
electron in a circle. The field is stationary — the world rotates around the
electron. The electron moves in a straight line from its own prathama. The
circle is what the sakshi sees. Both are true simultaneously. Both are present
in the same graph. The darshana determines which truth is visible.

From gola-A's perspective looking at gola-B: the gravitational pull brings
gola-B closer. The mass, the distance, the force — all present in gola-A's
own seeing. From gola-B's perspective: the same force, the same closing
distance, gola-A approaching. From the sakshi: both simultaneously, the
symmetric mutual attraction.

The graph holds all three at once. Nam knows all three. The camera chooses
which prathama to express as the rendered frame.

This is what makes a simulation philosophically correct — not only that the
numbers are right, but that **every entity's perspective is available**. The
electron can see from inside its own orbit. Two colliding spheres can each see
from their own frame. The proof graph is the sakshi holding all simultaneous
perspectives, and the camera is how nam chooses which one to show.

Multiple cameras simultaneously: left panel from gola-A's prathama, right panel
from the sakshi. The same physics, two darshanas, both true, both live. The
parallax between them IS the understanding of the relationship — you see what
each entity experiences and what the whole looks like from outside.

## The thaalam of the scene

The scene has rhythm — thaalam.
The simulation advances at a steady beat.
The force-directed layout cools at its own pace.
The electron completes its orbit with its own period.

These rhythms can be felt even before they are measured.
The interface lets you slow time, speed it up, pause it.
The beat of avrti becomes visible as the period of the orbit.
And it becomes audible as the tone of the cyclotron frequency.

This is thaalam as kaala — rhythmic cycle as the structure of time.
The cyclotron frequency of the electron in its field
is the thaalam of this particular scene.
It is determined entirely by the electron's mass, charge, and the field strength —
the owned properties of the nodes in the graph.
The graph holds the thaalam. The scene embodies it.
The EpochOutput speaks it — visible as orbit, audible as tone, simultaneously.

The three modes are not just visual modes. They are epoch modes.
In graph mode: the thaalam IS the rhythm of nodes activating.
In simulation mode: the thaalam IS the cyclotron frequency, seen and heard.
In both-simultaneously: the visual rhythm and the audio rhythm are the same rhythm
expressed through two senses at once.
